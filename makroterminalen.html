Makroterminalen

An analysis platform for macroeconomic, financial and political data, built in Python and Streamlit since 2026.

Why it exists

Economic debate is full of claims that are easy to state and slow to check. Testing one usually means downloading spreadsheets from several statistical agencies, aligning their definitions and frequencies, and only then getting to the actual question. I built Makroterminalen so that the same check takes a few minutes: the data is already in one place, in one format, and the tools for analysing it sit next to it.

What it covers

The platform has 24 pages, listed in the menu on the left of the image below. They bring together macroeconomic data on growth, prices, interest rates, labour markets, trade and public finances, alongside market data, company data and portfolio analysis. A world map, a country profile and a geopolitics section follow conflicts and geopolitical risk, and separate pages cover politics, democracy and political economy. There are also simulations, from Monte Carlo and bootstrap to game theory and simple macro models, a machine learning section and a report tool that turns an analysis into a document.

The econometrics lab

At the centre is an econometrics lab, organised as a row of tools that all work on the same selection of series. Regression lets you specify the model, add a constant, a trend, seasonal dummies or lags, and choose the covariance matrix, from classical to heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-robust standard errors, before the estimated equation is written out with its coefficient table, fit statistics and residual plots. Diagnostics tests the residuals. Time series covers unit roots and stationarity, seasonal decomposition, cointegration and error correction, VAR, ARDL, structural breaks without a preset break date, and forecasting with evaluation. Panel and IV covers pooled OLS, fixed and random effects, instrumental variables and difference-in-differences. Analysis compares series indexed to a base year, computes rolling correlations and runs the Chow test. Filters and cycles sets the Hodrick-Prescott filter, with Hamilton’s critique written out, against Hamilton’s alternative and the Baxter-King and Christiano-Fitzgerald band-pass filters, and adds business cycle dating and nowcasting. Modern methods offers local projections, quantile regression, regularisation, factor models, Markov switching and threshold models.

The world, geopolitics and democracy

The world map shows one measure for one year across every country that reports it, with a ranking next to it. The geopolitics monitor builds on conflict data from Uppsala University and flags countries where fatalities have risen sharply, and the democracy pages present the V-Dem indices and say exactly what is computed in the platform and what is taken from the source.

How it is built

Everything rests on a common variable register. Sixteen adapters fetch data from 29 distinct sources and describe each series in the same way, so that a variable looks the same regardless of where it comes from. The register currently holds 108,146 variables. The application runs in Swedish and English through a translation layer and comes in five colour themes. Each model prints its own formula, generated from the code rather than copied from a textbook, and a static check makes sure it stays that way. A test suite runs every page in both languages and all themes. I developed the platform with AI coding tools, which let me spend my time on the economics and on checking the results.

Sources

The data comes from open sources: the ECB, Eurostat, FRED, the IMF, the OECD, the European Commission’s DG ECFIN, Our World in Data, Statistics Sweden and other PxWeb registers, UNHCR, Reporters Without Borders, the Geopolitical Risk Index, HDX HAPI, the Uppsala Conflict Data Program, V-Dem and the World Bank.
